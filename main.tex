\section{Bridge}

In Chapter II, I use continuous space simulation and machine learning to evaluate evidence for co-geography in
the \textit{Anopheles-Plasmodium} host-parasite system, ultimately finding evidence for common dispersal patterns between
the parasite and its mosquito vector. 
Identifying patterns of spatial population structure and dispersal in this system, however, led me to ask further 
questions about how spatial structure may be shaping the evolutionary dynamics of these species.
How do beneficial alleles spread through these spatially-structured populations, and can we use spatial structure to detect them?
In Chapter III, I present my work investigating these questions, using a similar continuous-space simulation framework to uncover
a novel spatial signature of selective sweeps and then applying this observation to identify positively-selected alleles 
in a spatially-referenced genomic dataset of \textit{Anopheles gambiae} mosquitoes.

\section{Bridge}

As presented in Chapter III, spatial structure can be used to uncover adaptive variation at the population level, and indeed we successfuly identify
several candidate sweeping alleles in \textit{Anopheles gambiae}. 
However, \textit{Anopheles gambiae} is not the only malaria vector, and many \textit{Anopheles} species are segregating large amounts of potentially-adaptive variation.
In Chapter IV, I use a comparative approach to investigate the evolutionary forces shapping patterns of adaptive variation and divergence in 
three major \textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors.
This work reveals common selection pressures acting on these deeply-diverged vectors, with immune and metabolic pathways being particularly affected across a broad range of evolutionary timescales,
and highlights how local adaptation and diversifying selection maintain genetic variation in the same pathways.
