\chapter{Conclusion}

% Understanding the evolutionary dynamics of Plasmodium and its vectors is critical for effective vector control

% spatial pop gen can help inform this

In this dissertation, I use spatial patterns of variation in  
\textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors in order to 
uncover evolutionary dynamics that drive adaptation and divergence across their ranges.

In Chapter II, I present our work using correlated patterns of machine learning
prediction errors to infer common patterns of dispersal between \textit{Anopheles gambiae} 
mosquitoes and their \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} parasites. 
Applying \texttt{Locator} --- a machine learning method for predicting geographic origin from genetic data ---
to spatially-referenced genomic datasets of both vector and parasite,
I find that prediction errors are significantly correlated across space between the two species,
suggesting that they share common dispersal patterns.
Using continuous-space simulation, I investigate the circumstances that
can lead to biased errors in spatial prediction and develop methods 
to reduce these effects when applying this approach to empirical data.
After correcting for spatial bias in empirical sampling data,
correlations between \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} residuals persist,
providing evidence that these prediction errors reflect shared dispersal patterns 
between vector and parasite. This work uncovers previously unobserved co-geographic structure shared by
the malaria parasite and its vector on short evolutionary timescales, with 
implications for understanding disease transmission dynamics and vector control strategies in this system.

Given these findings, I became interested in how adaptive alleles
are spreading through these spatially-structured populations. In
Chapter III, I use continuous-space simulation of selective sweeps to 
discover a spatial signal of positive selection: I find that due to the 
wave-like nature of their spread, positively-selected alleles take up less
geographic area than neutral alleles at the same frequency.
Using this observation, I develop a method to 
detect adaptive alleles as 'space-frequency' outliers in spatially-structured populations.
Applying this method to \textit{Anopheles gambiae} populations in 
West Africa, I identify several 
novel alleles that appear to be under positive selection, including several 
functionally-relevant variants at loci associated with insecticide resistance.
This work demonstrates the potential of leveraging spatial population genetic structure to identify 
evolutionary processes, and the method I present here has relevance for
detecting adaptive alleles in a wide range of spatially-structured populations.
In \textit{Anopheles gambiae} in particular, this work provides insight into novel
genomic features that may be contributing to insecticide resistance and 
introduces a novel approach for identifying adaptive variants in this system.

\textit{Anopheles gambiae} is one of many \textit{Anopheles} species that transmit malaria,
and many of these species are segregating large amounts of potentially-adaptive variation.
In Chapter IV, I use a gene-focused comparative population genetic approach to 
investigate the evolutionary forces that shape patterns of variation and divergence in three 
major \textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors: African \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{funestus}, and Asian \textit{minimus}.
Focusing on patterns of nonsynonymous polymorphism and divergence
reveals common selection pressures acting on these deeply-diverged vectors to both maintain 
functional variation within and drive adaptive divergence between species, with immune and metabolic functions 
being particularly affected across a broad range of evolutionary timescales.
The biological functions that show evidence of both diversifying and directional selection 
are often shared across species and include many mechanisms of insecticide resistance, such as 
metabolic detoxification and cuticular resistance.
Investigating the spatial patterns of diversifying selection acting on \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{funestus},
I uncover two proteins involved in insecticide resistance that share convergent spatial and molecular patterns of variation,
demonstrating how local adaptation maintains functional variation in these relevant pathways.
This work provides insight into the parallel evolutionary forces that shape patterns of variation 
across these deeply-diverged malaria vectors, highlighting how the functional polymorphism maintained 
by local adaptation and balancing selection provides an abundant source of potentially-adaptive variation 
for rapid evolutionary responses to novel or intensified selection pressures.

Overall, this dissertation provides insight into how spatial population structure can be 
leveraged in genetic studies to uncover evolutionary phenomena in the \textit{Anopheles} malaria vector system
and beyond.
In Chapter II, I use correlated patterns of machine learning prediction errors to identify and confirm
co-geographic structure between \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{Plasmodium falciparum}, 
\comment{identifying} a previously undescribed pattern of shared dispersal between vector and parasite.
In Chapter III, I use continuous-space simulation to identify a novel spatial signature of selective sweeps
and apply this observation to identify positively-selected alleles in \textit{Anopheles gambiae}.
Finally, in Chapter IV, I use a comparative, gene-focused approach to investigate the evolutionary forces
shaping patterns of variation and divergence in three major \textit{Anopheles} vectors, 
uncovering common selection pressures across these deeply-diverged species and identifying 
convergent local adaptation to insecticide pressures.
The novel methods and approaches I present in this dissertation have relevance for a wide range of 
spatially-structured populations, and the insights into the \textit{Anopheles} system I present here
further our understanding of these important vectors and may inform future vector control strategies.
