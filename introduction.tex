\comment{To understand past evolutionary events, population genetics relies on analysis of
 patterns of genetic diversity that we observe in the present.}
Different evolutionary processes imprint different signatures on the genomes of populations:
Forces such as mutation, drift, and selection, as well as events such as population size changes or migration, 
will all drastically impact the patterns of genetic diversity that we observe in natural populations. 
For example, a beneficial mutation that sweeps to fixation will leave a characteristic pattern of reduced genetic diversity
by causing nearby linked loci to also increase in frequency alongside the beneficial allele \comment{CITATION}; 
or migration between two separate populations will lead to shared (introgressed) \comment{is this the right word?} regions
of the genome that have common patterns of variation between the populations.
The task of population genetics is to leverage these signatures in order to infer the evolutionary history they represent.

In natural populations, these patterns of genetic diversity are also distributed across geographic space, 
with each individual in a population - and their genome - occupying a location on the landscape. 
As evolutionary time plays forward, these individuals interact, reproduce, and disperse locally, giving rise to spatial 
genetic structure within populations: in short, individuals that are closer spatially tend to also be closer genetically.
This phenomenon, termed "isolation by distance" by Sewall Wright \comment{CITATION}, is ubiquitious in natural populations
and has been well-documented across a wide variety of taxa \comment{CITATIONS}.
However, despite its prevalence in natural populations, much of our contemporary understanding of
evolutionary processes is still informed by models that do not account for this spatial population structure \comment{CITATIONS}.
While the simplifying assumption that all individuals in a population are equally likely to mate regardless
of their spatial location makes evolutionary models more tractable, it obscures a key component of the population's
evolutionary history and can confound inference of evolutionary processes from genetic data \comment{CITATIONS}.
Further, understanding the relationship between spatial structure and 
observed patters of genetic diversity adds another dimension of information that can be leveraged to uncover
the evolutionary forces acting on populations. 
Spatial patterns of genetic variation have been successfully used to infer migration rates, estimate population sizes, and
detect selection \comment{CITATIONS};
however, the finer-scale patterns of spatial genetic structure now observable in genomic datasets remain unexplored in many contexts.as

In Chapter II, I combine a machine learning method for prediction of location from genotype data, called \texttt{Locator}, 
with forward-in-time, continuous-space, individual-based simulations to investigate and identify evidence for 
common patterns of dispersal between \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{Plasmodium falciparum}.
When using \texttt{Locator} to predict locations of \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum} from genotypic data, 
we observed similar patterns of prediction errors, which I discovered were significantly correlated between host and parasite across space. 
Because errors in predictions obtained using \texttt{Locator} tend to reflect the locations of a sample's ancestors, 
we hypothesized that the correlation in errors between \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum} 
may be due to common migration patterns - however, the observed correlation could also be due to spatial bias in the distribution
of sampling locations. After confirming that spatial bias in sampling locations can indeed lead to biased predictions,
I developed a sample weighting method to correct for this bias and reduce the influence of training set distributions on predictions.
Significant correlations between prediction errors persist 
when this method is applied to \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum}, providing further evidence for
these predictions reflecting shared patterns of dispersal across the landscape, particularly in West Africa.
This observation of common dispersal patterns between host and parasite across short evolutionary timescales is novel 
and has important implications for understanding patterns of gene flow and the spread of adaptive alleles in both species.

Positive selection plays a fundamental role in shaping variation in natural populations, and the 
rapid spread of beneficial alleles (a process called a "selective sweep") is responsible for many instances of adaptation.
This evolutionary process is particularly important to understand in the context of malaria control efforts, as both the parasite
and its vector are rapidly evolving to counter human interventions such as insecticides and antimalarial drugs.
While the impact of spatial population structure on the spread of beneficial mutations has been understood theoretically \comment{fisher citation},
much of our contemporary understanding of spatial selective sweeps is in regards to how spatial structure obscures 
the signatures of selection that we look for in genetic datasets \comment{citations}
In Chapter III, I use continuous-space simulation to identify a spatial signature of positive selection, then use this
observation to develop a method to identify sweeping alleles in spatially-referenced genetic datasets. 
In simulations of selective sweeps, I observed that positively-selected alleles tend to occupy less of the landscape than
neutral alleles at the same frequency. 
While counterintuitive, this \comment{observation} can be explained by the fact that neutral alleles, if not lost,  
will spread through a population - and across the landscape - without increasing in frequency.
Positively-selected alleles, on the other hand, will inherently increase in frequency as they sweep through the population, and this 
increase in frequency outpaces their spatial spread. 
Using this \comment{observation}, I develop an approach to detect positively-selected alleles as outliers to the 
genome-wide spatial-frequency distribution of genetic variants.
Applying this approach to \textit{Anopheles gambiae}, we identify multiple positively selected and functionally impactful 
variants segregating the population, including variants within known insecticide-resistant loci, 
potentially novel sources of insecticide resistance, and other potentially selected variants
associated with immune response, sensory perception, and development.
This work provides a novel method for identifying positively-selected alleles in spatially-structured populations and demonstrates
the potential of spatial information to inform, rather than obscure, our understanding of evolutionary processes.
