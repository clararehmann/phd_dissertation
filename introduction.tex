\chapter{Introduction}

The study of evolutionary histories and processes is essential to our understanding 
of the natural world and can help inform what will happen in the future.
Knowing what happened in the past and how it happened, however, requires inferring 
these processes from what we observe in the present.
To understand past evolutionary events, population genetics relies on the analysis of
patterns of genetic diversity in natural populations.
Different evolutionary processes imprint different signatures on the genomes of populations:
Forces such as mutation, drift, and selection, as well as events such as population size changes or migration, 
will all drastically impact the patterns of genetic diversity in natural populations. 
For example, a beneficial mutation that rises to fixation will leave a characteristic pattern 
of reduced diversity in the genome in a process known as a "selective sweep," \citep{smith_hitch-hiking_1974},
or migration between two separate populations will lead to shared regions
of the genome that have common patterns of variation between the populations.
The goal of population genetics is to identify and leverage these genetic signatures in order to 
infer the evolutionary history they represent.

In natural populations, these patterns of genetic diversity are also distributed across geographic space, 
with each individual in a population - and their genome - occupying a location on the landscape. 
As evolutionary time plays forward, these individuals interact, reproduce, and disperse locally, giving rise to spatial 
genetic structure within populations: in short, individuals that are closer spatially tend to also be closer genetically.
This phenomenon, termed "isolation by distance" by Sewall Wright \citep{wright1946isolation}, is ubiquitous in natural populations
and has been well-documented across a wide variety of taxa \citep{xue2021discovery, schlenke2004strong}.
However, despite its prevalence in natural populations, much of our contemporary understanding of
evolutionary processes is still informed by models that do not account for this spatial population structure.
While the simplifying assumption that all individuals in a population are equally likely to mate regardless
of their spatial location makes evolutionary models more tractable, it obscures a key component of the population's
evolutionary history and can confound inference of evolutionary processes from genetic data \citep{bradburd2019spatial}.
Further, understanding the relationship between spatial structure and 
observed patterns of genetic diversity adds another dimension of information that can be leveraged to uncover
the evolutionary forces acting on populations. 
Spatial patterns of genetic variation have been successfully used to infer migration rates, estimate population sizes, and
detect selection \citep{Muktupavela_Petr_Ségurel_Korneliussen_Novembre_Racimo_2022, Patterson_Goodfellow_Ting_Kern_Ralph_2026, Smith_Patterson_Ralph_Kern_2024};
however, the finer-scale patterns of spatial genetic structure now observable in genomic datasets remain unexplored in many contexts.



This dissertation focuses on leveraging spatial population structure to uncover and describe evolutionary processes 
in the malaria mosquito vector, \textit{Anopheles}.
Malaria is an ancient human disease that remains a persistent public health threat despite recent advances in 
treatments, vaccines, and vector control efforts.
Both the parasite and its vector rapidly evolve to counter human interventions, making understanding their 
evolutionary histories and trajectories crucial to effective disease prevention strategies.
Vector control efforts in particular are critical for containing the spread of malaria,
and extensive genomic surveillance of \textit{Anopheles} species now allows us to not only understand 
the population structure of these mosquitoes, but also to identify when and how they are adapting to human interventions
such as insecticides \citep{anopheles2017genetic, consortium_genome_2020}.
Using extensive spatial population genomic datasets of \textit{Anopheles} mosquitoes, I 
investigate patterns of dispersal, selection, polymorphism, and divergence in this system,
focusing on how incorporating spatial information into genetic analyses helps uncover processes that would otherwise go undetected.

In Chapter II, I combine a machine learning method for prediction of location from genotype data, called \texttt{Locator}, 
with forward-in-time, continuous-space, individual-based simulations to investigate and identify evidence for 
common patterns of dispersal between \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{Plasmodium falciparum}.
When using \texttt{Locator} to predict locations of \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum} from genotypic data, 
we observed similar patterns of prediction errors, which I discovered were significantly correlated between host and parasite across space. 
Because errors in predictions obtained using \texttt{Locator} tend to reflect the locations of a sample's ancestors, 
we hypothesized that the correlation in errors between \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum} 
may be due to common migration patterns - however, the observed correlation could also be due to spatial bias in the distribution
of sampling locations. After confirming that spatial bias in sampling locations can indeed lead to biased predictions,
I developed a sample weighting method to correct for this bias and reduce the influence of training set distributions on predictions.
Significant correlations between prediction errors persist 
when this method is applied to \textit{An. gambiae} and \textit{P. falciparum}, providing further evidence for
these predictions reflecting shared patterns of dispersal across the landscape, particularly in West Africa.
This observation of common dispersal patterns between host and parasite across short evolutionary timescales is novel 
and has important implications for understanding patterns of gene flow and the spread of adaptive alleles in both species.
This work was published in \textit{G3 | Genes | Genomes | Genetics} in 2024 and is co-authored by 
Peter L. Ralph and Andrew D. Kern.


Investigating patterns of dispersal in \textit{Anopheles} and \textit{Plasmodium}, I became interested in how beneficial variants
were spreading through these spatially-structured populations.
Positive selection plays a fundamental role in shaping variation in natural populations, and the 
rapid spread of beneficial alleles is responsible for many instances of adaptation.
This evolutionary process is particularly important to understand in the context of malaria control efforts, as both the parasite
and its vector are rapidly evolving to counter human interventions such as insecticides and antimalarial drugs.
While the impact of spatial population structure on the spread of beneficial mutations has been understood theoretically \citep{fisher_wave_1937},
much of our contemporary understanding of spatial selective sweeps is in regards to how spatial structure obscures 
the signatures of selection that we look for in genetic datasets \citep{chotai_signatures_2024, Min_Gupta_Desai_Weissman_2022}.
In Chapter III, I use continuous-space simulation to identify a spatial signature of positive selection, then use this
observation to develop a method to identify sweeping alleles in spatially-referenced genetic datasets. 
In simulations of selective sweeps, I observed that positively-selected alleles tend to occupy less of the landscape than
neutral alleles at the same frequency. 
While counterintuitive, this observation can be explained by the fact that neutral alleles, if not lost,  
will spread through a population - and across the landscape - without increasing in frequency.
Positively-selected alleles, on the other hand, will inherently increase in frequency as they sweep through the population, and this 
increase in frequency outpaces their spatial spread. 
Using this observation, I develop an approach to detect positively-selected alleles as outliers to the 
genome-wide spatial-frequency distribution of genetic variants.
Applying this approach to \textit{Anopheles gambiae}, we identify multiple positively selected and functionally impactful 
variants segregating the population, including variants within known insecticide-resistant loci, 
potentially novel sources of insecticide resistance, and other potentially selected variants
associated with immune response, sensory perception, and development.
This work provides a novel method for identifying positively-selected alleles in spatially-structured populations and demonstrates
the potential of spatial information to inform, rather than obscure, our understanding of evolutionary processes.
This work was published in \textit{Molecular Biology and Evolution} in 2025 and is co-authored by Scott T. Small, Peter L. Ralph, and Andrew D. Kern.

\textit{Anopheles gambiae} exists as part of a broader genus of malaria vectors, and in Chapter IV, I use 
a comparative approach to investigate the
evolutionary forces driving patterns of polymorphism and divergence in \textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors. 
Phylogenetic analysis of \textit{Anopheles} vectors has highlighted their rapid rate
of genome evolution, while population genomic analyses of \textit{Anopheles} species have revealed that 
these mosquitoes are incredibly polymorphic and segregating many potentially adaptive variants.
Focusing on nonsynonymous polymorphism and divergence in two major African vectors (\textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{funestus}) 
and one major Asian vector (\textit{Anopheles minimus}), 
I explore what genes and biological processes are evolving under directional selection in these species
and identify how patterns of polymorphism reflect similar evolutionary pressures.
I find that genes involved in sensory perception, immune response, and insecticide resistance are
both rapidly evolving under directional selection in all three species and segregating high amounts of nonsynonymous polymorphism,
suggesting that the variation maintained by \textit{Anopheles} populations may be important for adaptation to rapidly-changing
environments and selection pressures.
Using patterns of spatial differentiation in nonsynonymous polymorphism, I also identify candidate genes that may be adapting
to local selection pressures in both \textit{Anopheles gambiae} and \textit{Anopheles funestus}, including mutations with 
similar molecular impacts and spatial distributions in two 
cytochrome P450 enzymes, which are known to be involved in metabolic insecticide resistance.
My work in this chapter provides insight into the evolutionary forces shaping patterns of polymorphism and divergence in 
\textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors and highlights the importance of considering spatial population structure when investigating these processes.
This study is in preparation for submission to \textit{PLOS Genetics} and is co-authored by Andrew D. Kern.

Overall, my dissertation explores how spatial population structure can be leveraged to better understand evolutionary processes
in \textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors, 
with important implications for understanding how these species are adapting in response to human interventions.
Chapter II provides some of the first evidence for co-geography between \textit{Anopheles} and \textit{Plasmodium},
suggesting that these species are dispersing in similar trajectories and that their evolutionary histories are intertwined across space.
In Chapter III, I identify a novel spatial signature of positive selection and use this observation to develop and apply a method
for detecting positively-selected alleles in \textit{Anopheles gambiae}, identifying multiple novel candidate insecticide resistance alleles.
Finally, Chapter IV uses a comparative approach to identify and describe the evolutionary forces driving divergence and shaping
patterns of polymorphism across three major \textit{Anopheles} malaria vectors, highlighting how variable selection pressures
across space may be maintaining important variation in these species.
Together, this work demonstrates the potential of spatial information to inform our understanding of evolutionary processes
in natural populations and provides insight into the evolutionary dynamics of malaria parasites and their mosquito vectors.
